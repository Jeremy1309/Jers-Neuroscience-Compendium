Disease mechanisms

Neurodegenerative Diseases

A growing guide to neurodegenerative diseases, their biological mechanisms, and their effects on the nervous system.

01 · Orientation

Overview

Neurodegenerative diseases are disorders characterized by the progressive deterioration and loss of neurons. Unlike conditions that alter nervous-system function without widespread neuronal loss, neurodegenerative diseases are defined by degeneration within particular cell populations, pathways, or brain regions.

These diseases usually develop gradually and cause worsening changes in movement, memory, cognition, behaviour, or other neurological functions. Although each condition affects different cells and circuits, common mechanisms include abnormal protein accumulation, impaired cellular maintenance, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation.

Their causes are often complex. Some conditions are strongly associated with inherited mutations, while others reflect an interaction among aging, genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, and biological changes that accumulate over time. Aging is a major risk factor because cellular damage accumulates while mechanisms that maintain neuronal health become less effective.

Research has improved diagnosis and produced treatments that manage symptoms or modify aspects of disease progression. However, these conditions remain difficult to treat, and many still have no cure.

02 · Reference

Glossary

Terms used to describe degeneration, protein pathology, affected cells, and inheritance.

Neurodegeneration
Progressive damage to and loss of neurons or their supporting structures.
Lewy body
An abnormal intracellular aggregate rich in alpha-synuclein, commonly associated with Parkinson's disease and related disorders.
Alpha-synuclein
A neuronal protein that can misfold and accumulate abnormally in synucleinopathies.
Substantia nigra
A midbrain region containing dopamine-producing neurons that are especially affected in Parkinson's disease.
Chorea
Irregular, involuntary movements commonly associated with Huntington's disease.
Autosomal dominant
An inheritance pattern in which one altered copy of a gene can be sufficient to cause a condition.
Amyloid plaque
An extracellular deposit containing beta-amyloid protein fragments.
Tau tangle
An intracellular accumulation of abnormally modified tau protein.
Motor neuron
A nerve cell that controls voluntary muscles and contributes to functions such as movement, speech, swallowing, and breathing.
Demyelination
Damage to or loss of the myelin that insulates axons and supports efficient neural communication.

03 · Explore

Diseases

Each disease is defined by a different pattern of vulnerable cells, circuits, symptoms, and progression.

Parkinson's Disease

Parkinson's disease is a progressive disorder of the nervous system whose movement symptoms often develop gradually. Common motor features include resting tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia or slowed movement, and postural instability.

A central pathological feature is degeneration of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta. The resulting loss of dopamine disrupts basal-ganglia motor circuits, weakening pathways that facilitate desired movement and strengthening pathways that suppress movement.

Affected neurons commonly contain Lewy bodies enriched in abnormally aggregated alpha-synuclein. Parkinson's disease can also involve non-motor changes, including altered sleep, mood, cognition, and autonomic function.

Huntington's Disease

Huntington's disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder caused by an expanded CAG repeat in the HTT gene. The mutation is inherited in an autosomal-dominant pattern, meaning a child of an affected parent has a 50% chance of inheriting it.

The disease prominently affects GABAergic medium spiny neurons in the striatum. Early disruption of the indirect basal-ganglia pathway reduces movement suppression and contributes to chorea and other excessive involuntary movements.

Huntington's disease can produce movement abnormalities, cognitive decline, mood changes, and changes in personality or behaviour. Genetic testing can identify the expanded gene, but there is currently no cure.

Alzheimer's Disease

Alzheimer's disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the most common cause of dementia. Its pathology includes extracellular beta-amyloid plaques and intracellular tangles formed from abnormally modified tau protein.

These changes begin in regions important for learning and memory and spread through connected brain networks. As neurons and their connections are lost, the cortex and other affected structures undergo increasing atrophy.

Symptoms typically progress from difficulty forming or retrieving memories to broader changes in reasoning, language, orientation, behaviour, and the ability to perform everyday activities.

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive disorder that damages motor neurons in the brain and spinal cord. As these neurons degenerate, muscles receive less neural input and become weak, begin to twitch, and eventually atrophy.

Progression can interfere with walking, speaking, swallowing, and breathing while sensation is often relatively preserved. ALS may occur sporadically or run in families, and its rate of progression varies among individuals.

Multiple Sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis is an immune-mediated disease in which inflammation damages myelin and other components of the central nervous system. This disrupts communication between the brain, spinal cord, and the rest of the body, producing neurological symptoms that vary according to the location and extent of damage.
