Neurological & Psychiatric Disorders

A neuroscience-focused framework for understanding conditions that affect cognition, emotion, behaviour, development, and physical function.

01 · Orientation

4.1 Overview

Abnormal behaviour and mental disturbances have been observed throughout human history. Before the development of modern medicine and neuroscience, many cultures attributed these experiences to supernatural or spiritual forces such as possession, witchcraft, or demonic influence. Responses were often led by religious or spiritual figures rather than guided by biological evidence.

As neuroscience and psychiatry developed during the twentieth century, researchers increasingly investigated how brain structure, development, signalling, and experience contribute to mental and neurological conditions. Some early interventions were based on incomplete or incorrect models. Convulsive therapies developed in the 1930s partly from a mistaken belief that epilepsy and schizophrenia were biologically antagonistic. Modern electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is very different from its early forms: it is performed under anaesthesia with medical monitoring and remains an evidence-based option for certain severe or treatment-resistant conditions.

A mental or neurological disorder is a condition in which disruptions to brain structure, function, development, or signalling contribute to significant changes in cognition, emotion, behaviour, or physical function. These conditions can arise through interacting genetic, biological, environmental, developmental, and psychological factors.

The first Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) was published in 1952 to provide a shared framework for diagnosis and research. The current edition is the DSM-5-TR, published in 2022 and supplemented by subsequent approved updates. It organizes diagnostic criteria and provides a common clinical language, but it is not itself a treatment guide.

Important distinction: disorders are a broad category involving abnormal nervous-system function, which may involve development, neurotransmission, injury, immune dysfunction, genetics, or other processes. Neurodegenerative diseases are a more specific group whose defining feature is progressive neuronal dysfunction, damage, and loss.

02 · Reference

4.2 Disorder Glossary

Core language used to describe clinical conditions, their causes, and their effects.

Diagnosis
The process of identifying which illness or condition best explains a person's presentation.
Etiology
The causes or origins of a disease or disorder.
Pathophysiology
The biological mechanisms and functional changes that contribute to the development of a disorder.
Symptom
A subjective experience reported by an individual, such as hallucinations or memory difficulties.
Sign
An observable or measurable indicator, such as a tremor or an abnormal brain-imaging result.
Risk factor
A characteristic or exposure associated with an increased likelihood of developing a condition.
Prevalence
The proportion of a population living with a disorder during a defined period.
Incidence
The number of new cases that develop within a population over a defined period.
Comorbidity
The presence of multiple disorders or health conditions in the same individual.
Treatment
An intervention intended to reduce symptoms, improve function, or alter disease progression.

4.3 Trisomy 21 (Down Syndrome)

Overview. Down syndrome, also known as trisomy 21, is a genetic condition caused by additional chromosome 21 material. In the most common form, cells contain three copies of chromosome 21 instead of two. This increases the expression of many genes and changes patterns of development throughout the body, including the development and function of the nervous system.

Down syndrome is the most common chromosomal cause of intellectual disability. Development varies considerably between individuals but can involve differences in learning, memory, language, and information processing. Associated physical and medical features also vary, and improvements in health care and social support have substantially increased life expectancy and quality of life.

Cause. Most cases result from nondisjunction, an error during cell division in which chromosome 21 fails to separate normally. There are three principal chromosomal forms:

  • Trisomy 21: three separate copies of chromosome 21 are present in nearly all cells; this accounts for most cases.
  • Translocation Down syndrome: additional chromosome 21 material is attached to another chromosome.
  • Mosaic Down syndrome: some cells contain three copies of chromosome 21 while others contain the usual two.

The characteristic clinical presentation was described by John Langdon Down in 1866, and its chromosomal basis was identified in 1959.

Neurobiology. Additional chromosome 21 material alters the expression of many genes during development and affects the formation and function of neural circuits. Down syndrome is associated with differences in brain development and with elevated rates of certain neurological and systemic conditions, including epilepsy, congenital heart defects, hearing difficulties, sleep apnea, and immune dysfunction.

One important neurological association is Alzheimer's disease. Chromosome 21 contains the gene encoding amyloid precursor protein (APP), so an additional copy increases APP gene dosage. This contributes to a high likelihood of developing Alzheimer-related amyloid and tau neuropathology with age, although the age and clinical expression of dementia vary.

Diagnosis and support. Prenatal screening estimates the likelihood of Down syndrome but does not by itself establish a diagnosis. Diagnostic chromosome testing can be performed prenatally, and a postnatal karyotype can confirm additional chromosome 21 material and identify the chromosomal form.

There is currently no treatment that removes the underlying chromosomal difference. Care focuses on early developmental and educational support, treatment of associated health conditions, and promoting participation, independence, and quality of life.

4.4 Schizophrenia

Overview. Schizophrenia is a chronic psychiatric disorder that can affect perception, thought, emotion, motivation, cognition, and behaviour. Symptoms often become clinically apparent during late adolescence or early adulthood, although subtle changes may occur earlier. Schizophrenia does not mean "split personality"; its defining features can include psychosis and difficulty accurately interpreting reality.

Symptoms are commonly discussed across three broad domains:

  • Positive symptoms: experiences added to ordinary functioning, including hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized thought or speech.
  • Negative symptoms: reductions in ordinary functioning, such as decreased motivation, diminished emotional expression, social withdrawal, and reduced interest or pleasure.
  • Cognitive symptoms: difficulties involving attention, working memory, learning, processing speed, and executive function.

Cause. Schizophrenia does not have one single cause. It is thought to arise from interactions among many genetic variants and environmental or developmental factors. Prenatal or birth complications, early adversity, and exposure to certain substances may influence risk, but no single factor is sufficient to explain the disorder.

Neurobiology. No single neurotransmitter abnormality explains schizophrenia. One influential model proposes excessive dopamine signalling in mesolimbic circuits in relation to positive symptoms and reduced or inefficient dopamine signalling in mesocortical circuits in relation to negative and cognitive symptoms. Glutamate, GABA, and other signalling systems are also being studied.

At the systems level, research identifies differences in the development and connectivity of prefrontal, temporal, hippocampal, striatal, and thalamic networks. These findings support a distributed-circuit model rather than dysfunction in one isolated brain region.

Diagnosis and treatment. Diagnosis is clinical and considers symptoms, duration, functional impact, and alternative causes of psychosis. There is currently no single biological test that definitively diagnoses schizophrenia.

Treatment commonly combines antipsychotic medication with psychosocial interventions. Antipsychotics can reduce the intensity and frequency of psychotic symptoms but are often less effective for persistent negative and cognitive symptoms. Cognitive behavioural therapy, family education, supported education and employment, cognitive remediation, and coordinated specialty care can support recovery and functioning. Clozapine is an important option for treatment-resistant schizophrenia and requires regular medical monitoring.

4.5 Major Depressive Disorder

Overview. Major depressive disorder is characterized by persistent depressed mood and/or a marked loss of interest or pleasure, called anhedonia, together with additional emotional, cognitive, behavioural, and physical symptoms that significantly impair daily functioning. A major depressive episode lasts at least two weeks, with symptoms present for much of the day on most days.

Common features include low mood, hopelessness, fatigue, altered sleep or appetite, difficulty concentrating, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt, psychomotor slowing or agitation, and thoughts of death or suicide. Presentation and severity vary considerably between individuals.

Neurobiology. Depression is not adequately explained as simply "too little serotonin." Current research emphasizes interacting systems involved in mood, reward, stress, memory, and cognitive control. Frequently studied regions include the prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, hypothalamus, and ventral striatum.

Serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine, glutamate, and GABA all participate in these networks. Stress systems such as the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis can also be altered in some people. Contemporary models therefore focus on circuit function, synaptic plasticity, stress regulation, and gene–environment interaction rather than a single chemical deficiency.

Treatment. Evidence-based care can include psychotherapy, antidepressant medication, or both. Cognitive behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy are well-established approaches. Although many antidepressants rapidly alter monoamine signalling, clinical improvement usually develops more slowly, suggesting that downstream changes in circuits and plasticity are important.

For severe or treatment-resistant depression, clinicians may consider approaches such as ECT, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), or ketamine- or esketamine-based treatment when medically appropriate. Treatment selection depends on symptom severity, safety, previous response, other health conditions, and individual preferences.

4.6 Bipolar Disorder

Overview. Bipolar disorder is defined by episodes of abnormally elevated or irritable mood together with increased energy or activity. These occur as mania or the less severe state called hypomania. Depressive episodes are also common and may occupy a substantial portion of the illness.

During mania, a person may experience decreased need for sleep, racing thoughts, increased speech, inflated self-confidence or grandiosity, distractibility, increased goal-directed activity, and impulsive or risky behaviour. Severe mania causes marked functional impairment and may include psychotic symptoms.

Major types.

  • Bipolar I disorder: defined by at least one manic episode; a major depressive episode may also occur but is not required for the diagnosis.
  • Bipolar II disorder: involves hypomanic episodes and major depressive episodes, without a history of a full manic episode.
  • Cyclothymic disorder: involves recurring periods of hypomanic and depressive symptoms that do not meet full episode criteria.

Neurobiology. Bipolar disorder is strongly influenced by genetics, but no single gene determines it. Research suggests altered regulation within networks connecting the prefrontal cortex with limbic and reward-related regions, including the amygdala and ventral striatum. Dopamine and other monoamines, glutamate–GABA balance, intracellular signalling, circadian rhythms, sleep regulation, and synaptic plasticity have all been implicated. These are network-level models rather than definitive single causes.

Treatment. Treatment is usually long term and may combine mood-stabilizing medicines, atypical antipsychotics, psychotherapy, and careful management of sleep and daily rhythms. Lithium has strong evidence for preventing mood episodes and can reduce suicide risk, while valproate and several atypical antipsychotics are used in appropriate clinical situations.

Antidepressants may sometimes be added for bipolar depression but are generally not used alone because they can precipitate mania or rapid cycling in susceptible individuals. Medication choice and monitoring require individualized medical care.

4.7 Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

Overview. Obsessive-compulsive disorder is characterized by obsessions, compulsions, or both. Obsessions are recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images, or urges that cause distress. Compulsions are repetitive behaviours or mental acts a person feels driven to perform, often to reduce distress or prevent a feared outcome.

The relief produced by a compulsion is typically temporary, which can reinforce a repeating obsession–anxiety–compulsion cycle. OCD differs from ordinary preferences for neatness, organization, or routine because its symptoms are difficult to control, time-consuming, distressing, or functionally impairing.

Neurobiology. OCD is associated with altered function in cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuits. These loops connect frontal regions, particularly the orbitofrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex, with the striatum and thalamus before returning to the cortex. They help evaluate potential threats or errors, select actions, form habits, and determine when a behaviour should stop.

In OCD, activity in parts of this system may become overly persistent, making intrusive concerns difficult to dismiss and repetitive actions difficult to terminate. Serotonin is clinically relevant because serotonin reuptake inhibitors can reduce symptoms, but glutamate, dopamine, and other signalling systems also contribute to CSTC function.

Treatment. A first-line psychological treatment is exposure and response prevention (ERP), a specialized form of cognitive behavioural therapy. A person gradually encounters triggers in a controlled, collaborative setting while refraining from the usual compulsion, allowing anxiety and threat expectations to change without relying on the ritual.

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are also commonly used. OCD may require a longer treatment trial than depression, with dosing determined by a qualified prescriber. For severe treatment-resistant OCD, clinicians may consider certain forms of transcranial magnetic stimulation or, in carefully selected cases, deep brain stimulation.

4.8 Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

Overview. PTSD can develop after exposure to a traumatic event, although most people exposed to trauma do not develop the disorder. Diagnosis requires a defined pattern of trauma-related symptoms that persists for more than one month and causes clinically significant distress or impairment.

Symptoms span four major domains: intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance, persistent negative changes in cognition and mood, and heightened arousal or reactivity. Examples include flashbacks or nightmares, avoiding reminders, persistent guilt or negative beliefs, emotional detachment, exaggerated startle, irritability, hypervigilance, and sleep or concentration difficulties.

Neurobiology of fear and memory. PTSD involves systems that learn, store, retrieve, and regulate threat-related information. The amygdala contributes to detecting emotionally significant stimuli and generating defensive responses. The hippocampus supplies contextual and episodic information, helping distinguish past danger from the present environment. Medial prefrontal regions contribute to regulating emotion and to fear extinction, the learning process through which a previously threatening cue is recognized as safe.

Altered communication among these regions can contribute to persistent threat responses after immediate danger has passed. Norepinephrine and autonomic arousal support vigilance and physiological responses to threat, while the HPA axis coordinates hormonal stress responses. There is no single biological abnormality shared by everyone with PTSD. Genetics, prior trauma, the severity and duration of exposure, social support, and post-trauma stressors can all influence vulnerability and recovery.

Treatment. Evidence-based care often uses trauma-focused psychotherapy. Approaches may include gradual, supported exposure to trauma memories or reminders and cognitive restructuring of persistent beliefs involving danger, guilt, or shame. The aim is to reduce avoidance and update threat predictions rather than erase the memory.

Certain medicines, including some SSRIs, can reduce symptoms for some individuals. Care is most effective when it also addresses current safety, co-occurring conditions, sleep, substance use, and social support. Recovery is possible, and treatment should be guided by a qualified health professional.
