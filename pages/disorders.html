Diseases & Disorders

A neuroscience-focused framework for neurological conditions, psychiatric disorders, and brain injury.

01 · Orientation

Overview

Abnormal behaviour and mental disturbances have been observed throughout human history. Before modern medicine and neuroscience, many cultures attributed these conditions to supernatural or spiritual causes, and responses were often shaped by religious or cultural beliefs rather than biological evidence.

As neuroscience and psychiatry developed during the twentieth century, researchers increasingly investigated how brain structure, development, signalling, and experience contribute to mental and neurological conditions. Some early interventions were based on incomplete models of brain function; modern practice relies on controlled research, clinical standards, and continuing evaluation of benefits and risks.

A mental or neurological disorder is a condition in which disruptions to brain structure, function, development, or signalling contribute to significant changes in cognition, emotion, behaviour, or physical function. These conditions can arise from interacting genetic, biological, environmental, and psychological factors.

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders was first published in 1952 to create a shared framework for diagnosis and research. Its current text revision, the DSM-5-TR, was published in 2022 and continues to be updated as evidence and clinical understanding evolve.

Disorders are a broad category involving abnormal nervous-system function. Neurodegenerative diseases are a more specific group in which progressive neuronal damage and loss are defining features.

02 · Reference

Glossary

Core language used to describe clinical conditions, their causes, and their effects.

Diagnosis
The process of identifying which illness or condition best explains a person's presentation.
Etiology
The causes or origins of a disease or disorder.
Pathophysiology
The biological mechanisms and functional changes that contribute to a disorder.
Symptom
A subjective experience or problem reported by an individual, such as hallucinations or memory difficulties.
Sign
An observable or measurable indicator, such as a tremor or an abnormal imaging result.
Risk factor
A characteristic or exposure associated with an increased likelihood of developing a condition.
Prevalence
The proportion of a population living with a disorder during a defined period.
Incidence
The number of new cases that occur within a population over a defined period.
Comorbidity
The presence of multiple disorders or health conditions in the same individual.
Treatment
An intervention intended to reduce symptoms, improve function, or alter disease progression.

03 · Explore

Subtopics

Conditions are organized around their biological basis, clinical effects, and place within neuroscience.

Trisomy 21 (Down Syndrome)

Down syndrome is a genetic condition caused by extra chromosome 21 material. In the most common form, trisomy 21, cells contain three copies of chromosome 21 rather than the usual two. The additional genetic material changes patterns of brain and body development.

People with Down syndrome can experience varying differences in learning, memory, language development, and cognitive processing. Neurologically, the condition is associated with differences in cortical development, synaptic connectivity, and regions involved in learning and memory, including the hippocampus.

Chromosome 21 contains the gene for amyloid precursor protein (APP). Increased APP expression helps explain why people with Down syndrome have an elevated likelihood of developing Alzheimer-related amyloid and tau pathology later in life.

There is no single treatment that removes the underlying chromosomal difference, but early intervention, educational support, appropriate health care, and therapies targeting developmental skills can improve independence and quality of life.

Psychiatric Disorders

Future chapters will address schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder. Each involves distinct diagnostic criteria and a different interaction of neural circuits, biology, experience, and environment.

A Clinical Framework

Understanding a disorder requires more than naming symptoms. A complete neuroscience account considers diagnosis, etiology, pathophysiology, risk factors, observable signs, subjective symptoms, comorbidities, and the evidence supporting available treatments.
