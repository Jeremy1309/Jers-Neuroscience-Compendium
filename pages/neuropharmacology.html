Drugs and the nervous system

Neuropharmacology

A structured guide to how drugs interact with neural signaling, receptors, and the nervous system.

01 · Orientation

Overview

Neuropharmacology examines how drugs alter nervous-system function through receptors, transporters, ion channels, enzymes, and other molecular targets. A drug's molecular action can lead to broader physiological and psychological effects that vary with dose, route of administration, metabolism, and individual biology.

Drugs may be administered orally, by inhalation, intramuscularly, intravenously, subcutaneously, or through specialized neuraxial routes such as epidural or intrathecal delivery. Intraperitoneal administration is also common in laboratory research. Route affects how quickly a drug reaches circulation and the brain, how much is available to tissues, and how long its effects last.

Metabolism chemically alters drugs, often reducing activity and making compounds easier to eliminate. Many transformations occur through enzymes in the liver, although the gastrointestinal tract, kidneys, blood, and other tissues can also contribute.

This page is an educational overview of mechanisms and should not be used as guidance for taking, combining, or stopping any substance or medication.

02 · Reference

Glossary

Foundational language for describing drug targets, responses, potency, and safety.

Drug action
The molecular change produced when a drug interacts with a target such as a receptor, transporter, ion channel, or enzyme.
Drug effect
The broader physiological or psychological response that follows a drug's molecular actions.
Ligand
A molecule that binds to a receptor or another biological target with some degree of selectivity.
Agonist
A ligand that activates a receptor and produces a biological response.
Antagonist
A ligand that occupies a receptor while blocking or reducing activation by other ligands.
Inverse agonist
A ligand that reduces a receptor's baseline activity and produces an effect opposite to that of an agonist.
Affinity
The strength of attraction between a ligand and its binding site.
ED50
The dose that produces a defined therapeutic effect in 50% of a studied population.
TD50
The dose that produces a defined toxic effect in 50% of a studied population.
Therapeutic index
A comparison between toxic and effective doses that provides one estimate of a drug's margin of safety.
Drug metabolism
Enzymatic modification of a drug, often making it less active or easier for the body to eliminate.

03 · Explore

Drug Classes and Mechanisms

These sections distinguish related substances by their targets, neural effects, and pharmacological profiles.

Psychostimulants: Amphetamines and Cocaine

Psychostimulants include both prescribed and non-medical substances that increase arousal and sympathetic activity. Their effects can include increased alertness and energy, elevated heart rate and blood pressure, reduced appetite, insomnia, and feelings of euphoria.

Amphetamine-type stimulants raise extracellular monoamine levels by entering presynaptic neurons through monoamine transporters, disrupting vesicular storage, and promoting reverse transport or release of dopamine and norepinephrine. Their exact effects depend on the compound and dose.

Cocaine is a psychostimulant but is not an amphetamine. It blocks monoamine transporters, including the dopamine transporter, which slows reuptake and increases neurotransmitter signalling in the synaptic cleft. Cocaine also blocks voltage-gated sodium channels, accounting for its local-anesthetic properties.

Methamphetamine and MDMA

Methamphetamine is structurally related to amphetamine but includes an additional methyl group that increases lipid solubility and supports rapid entry into the brain. It strongly increases dopamine and norepinephrine signalling and generally has a longer duration of action than cocaine.

MDMA, or 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine, is a substituted amphetamine with prominent effects on serotonin as well as dopamine and norepinephrine. Products sold as ecstasy or molly may contain MDMA, other substances, or variable mixtures, so the label does not reliably identify composition.

Opioids

Endogenous opioid peptides help regulate pain, reward, mood, stress, feeding, and other physiological processes. Opium is material obtained from the opium poppy, opiates are compounds derived directly from it, and opioid is the broader term for natural, semi-synthetic, synthetic, and endogenous compounds that act at opioid receptors.

Within reward circuitry, opioids can reduce GABA release onto dopamine-producing neurons in the ventral tegmental area. This disinhibition can increase dopamine signalling to regions such as the nucleus accumbens.

At the cellular level, opioid receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors. Their activation can open K+ channels, close voltage-gated Ca2+ channels, reduce neurotransmitter release, and inhibit adenylyl cyclase signalling.

  • Mu opioid receptor (MOR) — contributes to analgesia, reward, respiration, feeding, nausea, and other effects.
  • Delta opioid receptor (DOR) — contributes to pain modulation, motor integration, reinforcement, and cognition.
  • Kappa opioid receptor (KOR) — contributes to pain regulation, dysphoria, gut motility, and neuroendocrine function.
  • Nociceptin receptor (NOP) — participates in pain, feeding, learning, and neuroendocrine regulation but has a distinct endogenous ligand system.

Examples include morphine, heroin, codeine, oxycodone, fentanyl, and several endogenous peptides. Their pharmacokinetics and receptor activity differ, but respiratory depression is a major risk associated with opioid exposure.

Cannabis and Cannabinoids

The endocannabinoid system uses signalling molecules such as anandamide to activate cannabinoid receptors. CB1 receptors are common on presynaptic terminals, where their activation can reduce the release of other neurotransmitters and modify local circuit activity.

THC can activate cannabinoid receptors and produce the characteristic intoxicating effects associated with cannabis. CBD has a different pharmacological profile and does not produce the same intoxicating effect. Cannabinoid effects depend on dose, formulation, route, receptor distribution, and the neural circuit involved.

Psychedelic Drugs

Psychedelic drugs can produce marked changes in perception, cognition, emotion, and sense of self without necessarily producing a state of toxic delirium. Their mechanisms vary across chemical classes; many classic psychedelics act primarily through serotonin 5-HT2A receptors.

Potency, duration, physiological effects, and behavioural effects depend on the specific compound, dose, route, and individual. Research is examining both their risks and their potential therapeutic uses under controlled clinical conditions.
