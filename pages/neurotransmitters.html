Chemical communication

Neurotransmitters

A focused guide to neurotransmitters, receptors, and the chemical signalling systems that shape neural communication.

01 · Orientation

3.1 Overview

Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that allow neurons to communicate with one another and with other cells throughout the body. Neuronal communication involves both electrical and chemical processes. First, an electrical signal called an action potential travels along a neuron's axon toward the terminal bouton. When it reaches the presynaptic terminal, it triggers the release of neurotransmitters into the synapse, where they bind to specific receptors on the target cell.

Receptor binding can alter the activity of the receiving cell. Depending on the neurotransmitter, receptor type, and location within the nervous system, the response may increase or decrease cellular activity. Depolarizing signals can bring a neuron closer to firing and produce an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP), while hyperpolarizing or stabilizing signals can make firing less likely and produce an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP).

Neurons are often classified by the chemical messenger they primarily release: dopaminergic neurons release dopamine, glutamatergic neurons release glutamate, and GABAergic neurons release GABA. Although many neurons primarily use one neurotransmitter, some can release multiple neurotransmitters or neuromodulators through a process called co-transmission.

Important note: neurotransmitters rarely have one universal function. Their effects depend on receptor subtype, location, concentration, timing, the receiving cell, and the neural circuit involved. The distinction between the central and peripheral nervous systems adds another layer of complexity: a messenger's function is determined not by the molecule alone, but by the biological system in which it operates.

02 · Reference

3.2 Neurotransmitter Glossary

Key terms for chemical communication and receptor-based signalling.

Synapse (synaptic cleft)
The microscopic gap between the presynaptic neuron and postsynaptic cell where neurotransmitters are released and transmitted.
Presynaptic neuron
The neuron that releases neurotransmitters into the synapse.
Postsynaptic neuron
The neuron or target cell that receives and binds neurotransmitters.
Receptors
Proteins on the surface or inside of cells that bind specific molecules, such as neurotransmitters, hormones, or drugs, to produce a cellular response.
Excitatory neurotransmitter
A neurotransmitter that increases the likelihood that a postsynaptic neuron will generate an action potential by promoting neuronal activation; glutamate is a major example.
Inhibitory neurotransmitter
A neurotransmitter that decreases the likelihood that a postsynaptic neuron will generate an action potential by reducing neuronal activity; GABA is a major example.
Neuromodulator
A chemical messenger that modifies the activity of neurons or neural circuits rather than directly causing rapid excitation or inhibition; dopamine is one example.
Agonist
A molecule that binds to and activates a receptor, mimicking or enhancing the effects of a neurotransmitter.
Antagonist
A molecule that binds to a receptor but blocks or reduces its activation, preventing or decreasing the effects of a neurotransmitter.
Reuptake
The process by which neurotransmitters are transported back into the presynaptic neuron after release so they can be recycled or broken down.
Synaptic vesicles
Small membrane-bound structures within the terminal bouton that store neurotransmitters before they are released into the synapse.

03 · Explore

Major Neurotransmitters and Neuromodulators

An introduction to major chemical messengers, their receptors, and their roles across the nervous system.

3.3 Dopamine (DA)

Dopamine is a catecholamine neurotransmitter involved in movement, motivation, reward, attention, learning, and hormone regulation. Calling it the brain's "reward chemical" is misleading: dopamine does not simply create pleasure. Its effect depends heavily on where it is released, which receptors are activated, and which neural circuit is involved.

Most dopamine in the central nervous system is produced by relatively small groups of neurons in the midbrain and hypothalamus. Despite their small numbers, these neurons send influential projections throughout the brain through four major pathways.

Mesolimbic pathway — reward and motivation: originates primarily in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and projects to the nucleus accumbens, amygdala, hippocampus, and other limbic regions. It supports motivation, reinforcement, reward learning, and the assignment of salience to stimuli. Drugs can manipulate this signalling and reinforce drug-seeking behaviour, making the pathway important to addiction research.

Nigrostriatal pathway — motor movement: originates primarily in the substantia nigra pars compacta and projects to the dorsal striatum, especially the caudate and putamen. It helps initiate and regulate voluntary movement through basal ganglia circuits. Degeneration of these dopaminergic neurons contributes to the bradykinesia, rigidity, and resting tremor of Parkinson's disease. Dopamine also interacts with circuits affected in Huntington's disease, although Huntington's is not simply a dopamine deficiency.

Mesocortical pathway — cognition and executive function: also begins primarily in the VTA but projects to the prefrontal cortex. It contributes to attention, working memory, motivation, decision-making, cognitive flexibility, and salience. Prefrontal dopamine works best within an optimal range; either too little or too much can impair performance. Altered signalling has been studied in schizophrenia, ADHD, depression, and other disorders affecting cognition and motivation.

Tuberoinfundibular pathway — hormone regulation: hypothalamic dopaminergic neurons project to the median eminence and release dopamine into the hypophyseal portal circulation. Dopamine then acts on the anterior pituitary to inhibit prolactin secretion, functioning here as a neuroendocrine signal rather than across a typical synapse.

Together, these pathways show why dopamine cannot be reduced to a single function. The same messenger can support movement, goal pursuit, attention, learning, and hormone regulation depending on the pathway being activated.

3.4 Glutamate

Glutamate is the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system and plays a critical role in neuronal communication, learning, memory, and synaptic plasticity. Its effects still depend on the receptor it activates and the circuit in which it functions.

AMPA receptors are fast-acting ionotropic receptors that allow Na+ influx, depolarizing the postsynaptic neuron. NMDA receptors require both glutamate binding and sufficient postsynaptic depolarization; once activated, they allow Ca2+ influx and initiate intracellular processes involved in strengthening synapses. Metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluRs) are G-protein-coupled receptors that regulate slower, longer-lasting changes in neuronal activity.

Glutamate is central to long-term potentiation (LTP), a cellular mechanism associated with learning and memory. During LTP, repeated activation of glutamatergic synapses strengthens the connection between neurons, in part by increasing the number and responsiveness of AMPA receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.

Excessive glutamate activity can be harmful. Overstimulation of glutamate receptors, particularly NMDA receptors, can cause excessive Ca2+ influx, cellular stress, and neuronal damage. This process, called excitotoxicity, is implicated in stroke, traumatic brain injury, and neurodegenerative disease.

Glutamate also works closely with GABA, the brain's primary inhibitory neurotransmitter. Both are connected through the glutamate–glutamine cycle, and together they help maintain the balance between neuronal excitation and inhibition.

Key mechanisms to remember:

  • Glutamate → AMPA activation → Na+ influx → depolarization
  • Depolarization removes the Mg2+ block → NMDA activation
  • NMDA activation → Ca2+ influx → synaptic strengthening (LTP)
  • Excess glutamate → excitotoxicity → neuronal damage

3.5 GABA

Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) is the brain's major inhibitory neurotransmitter. If glutamate is one of the brain's primary accelerators, GABA acts as a crucial braking system. This balance between excitation and inhibition supports coordinated movement, sleep, and the prevention of uncontrolled electrical activity such as seizures.

GABA is produced from glutamate by the enzyme glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) and is released by inhibitory interneurons and projection neurons throughout the brain. It reduces neuronal excitability and helps coordinate the timing of neural activity.

GABA-A receptors are fast-acting ionotropic receptors that form ligand-gated chloride channels. In mature neurons, increasing chloride conductance generally makes action potentials less likely through hyperpolarization or shunting inhibition. These receptors are targets for benzodiazepines, barbiturates, many anaesthetic agents, and alcohol, which enhance GABAergic signalling through different mechanisms and can produce reduced anxiety, sedation, impaired coordination, or loss of consciousness.

GABA-B receptors are slower metabotropic G-protein-coupled receptors. Their activation can open potassium channels and inhibit voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing neuronal excitability and neurotransmitter release over a longer timescale.

Together, these receptor systems help regulate anxiety, sleep, motor coordination, muscle tone, seizure prevention, and rhythmic neural activity. GABA does not simply turn neurons off; it carefully regulates when, where, and how strongly neural circuits become active.

3.6 Serotonin (5-HT)

Serotonin, or 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), is a monoamine neurotransmitter synthesized from the amino acid tryptophan. Most serotonergic neurons in the central nervous system are concentrated in the brainstem's raphe nuclei. Although relatively few in number, they send widespread projections to the cerebral cortex, limbic system, hypothalamus, basal ganglia, cerebellum, and spinal cord.

This distribution allows serotonin to influence mood, sleep, appetite, pain, impulse control, cognition, and emotional processing. Rather than acting as a simple "happiness chemical," it regulates many different circuits.

There are seven major serotonin receptor families, 5-HT1 through 5-HT7, containing numerous subtypes. Nearly all are G-protein-coupled receptors, with one important exception: 5-HT3 is a ligand-gated ion channel. Serotonin may therefore increase or decrease activity depending on its receptor and circuit.

Serotonin is important to the study and treatment of depression, anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder, migraine, and other conditions, but these cannot be reduced to a simple serotonin deficiency. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) block the serotonin transporter (SERT), increasing serotonin availability; their therapeutic effects develop gradually and involve changes in receptors and neural circuits.

3.7 Acetylcholine (ACh)

Acetylcholine plays major roles in both the central and peripheral nervous systems. It acts through two fundamentally different receptor classes: nicotinic receptors are ligand-gated ion channels that produce rapid responses, while muscarinic receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors that produce slower, longer-lasting effects. Depending on the receptor subtype and target cell, acetylcholine can increase or decrease cellular activity.

In the central nervous system, acetylcholine contributes to attention, learning, memory, arousal, and sleep–wake regulation. Cholinergic neurons in the basal forebrain and brainstem project widely. The nucleus basalis of Meynert provides input to the cerebral cortex, while the medial septal region provides important input to the hippocampus. Degeneration of basal forebrain cholinergic neurons contributes to cognitive impairment in Alzheimer's disease, and acetylcholinesterase inhibitors can provide symptomatic treatment for some patients by slowing acetylcholine breakdown.

In the peripheral nervous system, motor neurons release acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction, where nicotinic receptor activation initiates skeletal-muscle contraction. Acetylcholine is also released by every preganglionic autonomic neuron and by most postganglionic parasympathetic neurons, where it commonly acts on muscarinic receptors in target organs.

The same molecule can therefore help a person focus, contract a skeletal muscle, and regulate organ activity, depending on where it is released and which receptor it activates.

3.8 Norepinephrine (NE)

Norepinephrine, also called noradrenaline, is a catecholamine neurotransmitter associated with arousal, vigilance, attention, stress, and memory. The brain's primary source is the locus coeruleus, a small nucleus in the pons whose neurons project broadly to the cortex, hippocampus, cerebellum, thalamus, hypothalamus, and spinal cord.

Norepinephrine acts through alpha-1, alpha-2, and beta adrenergic receptors, all of which are G-protein-coupled receptors. Alpha-2 receptors can serve as autoreceptors, allowing noradrenergic neurons to detect released norepinephrine and reduce further release through feedback control.

More norepinephrine is not necessarily better. Moderate levels can enhance alertness, selective attention, and prefrontal function. During intense stress, very high catecholamine levels can shift brain activity away from flexible, deliberate prefrontal control toward faster, more reflexive or habitual responses.

This relationship with brain state makes norepinephrine relevant to ADHD, anxiety disorders, PTSD, and depression. Several medicines used to treat these conditions alter noradrenergic signalling.

3.9 Adenosine

Adenosine is a purine neuromodulator strongly involved in sleep pressure. Neurons continually use ATP to maintain ion gradients, transmit signals, and support cellular function. As ATP is broken down into ADP and AMP, further metabolism can produce adenosine.

Across prolonged wakefulness, extracellular adenosine signalling builds and generally suppresses neural activity, contributing to the increasing biological drive to sleep. Adenosine acts through four G-protein-coupled receptors: A1, A2A, A2B, and A3. In the brain, A1 receptor activation generally suppresses neuronal firing and transmitter release, while A2A signalling has important modulatory effects in regions such as the striatum.

Sleep reduces adenosine signalling and sleep pressure. Caffeine promotes wakefulness primarily by antagonizing A1 and A2A receptors. It does not remove the underlying need for sleep; it temporarily limits the brain's response to adenosine. As caffeine is metabolized, accumulated sleep pressure can become more noticeable, contributing to the familiar "caffeine crash."

3.10 Histamine

Histamine is well known for its role in allergies, but it also acts as an important neurotransmitter. In the central nervous system it helps maintain arousal, attention, and cortical activation, while contributing to appetite regulation, cognition, vestibular processing, and neuroimmune interactions.

Histamine acts through four G-protein-coupled receptor types: H1, H2, H3, and H4. H1 receptors are particularly important for wake-promoting effects. H3 receptors often serve as presynaptic autoreceptors and heteroreceptors, helping regulate both histamine release and the release of other neurotransmitters.

This connection with wakefulness explains why many first-generation antihistamines cause drowsiness: they can cross the blood–brain barrier and block central H1 receptors, reducing histamine-mediated arousal.

3.11 Oxytocin

Oxytocin is a neuropeptide that functions both as a hormone in the body and as a neuromodulator in the brain. During labour, cervical stretching promotes oxytocin release, which strengthens uterine contractions through a positive feedback loop. During breastfeeding, suckling stimulates oxytocin release and contraction of myoepithelial cells around the mammary glands, producing milk ejection; milk production itself is primarily regulated by prolactin.

Within the central nervous system, oxytocin influences social recognition, maternal behaviour, social salience, stress responses, and pair bonding in some species. The label "love hormone" is an oversimplification: its effects depend on social context, receptor distribution, previous experience, and the neural circuits involved.

3.12 Vasopressin

Vasopressin, also called antidiuretic hormone (ADH), is a neuropeptide that functions both as a hormone and a neuromodulator. Its major peripheral role is maintaining water balance. When blood becomes too concentrated, hypothalamic osmoreceptors detect the rise in plasma osmolality and stimulate vasopressin release.

Vasopressin acts on V2 receptors in the kidney's collecting ducts, promoting insertion of aquaporin-2 water channels into the cell membrane. More water is reabsorbed into the bloodstream, reducing urine volume and producing more concentrated urine. At higher concentrations, vasopressin can also activate V1a receptors on vascular smooth muscle, causing vasoconstriction and contributing to blood-pressure regulation.

Within the brain, vasopressin has been implicated in social recognition, aggression, stress responses, reproductive behaviour, and pair bonding, particularly in animal models. These effects vary with species, sex, receptor distribution, and social context, so vasopressin cannot be assigned one universal social function.
