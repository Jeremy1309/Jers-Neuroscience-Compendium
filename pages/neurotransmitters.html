Chemical communication

Neurotransmitters

A focused guide to neurotransmitters, receptors, and the chemical signaling systems that shape neural communication.

01 · Orientation

Overview

Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that allow neurons to communicate with each other and with other cells throughout the body. Neuronal communication occurs through both electrical and chemical processes. First, an electrical signal called an action potential travels along the neuron's axon toward the terminal bouton. When the action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal, it triggers the release of neurotransmitters into the synapse, where they bind to specific receptors on the target cell.

When neurotransmitters bind to receptors, they can alter the activity of the receiving cell. Depending on the neurotransmitter, receptor type, and location within the nervous system, this can either increase or decrease cellular activity. For example, glutamate typically promotes neuronal activation and plays an important role in learning and memory, while GABA generally reduces neuronal activity and helps prevent excessive excitation.

Changes in neuronal activity occur through alterations in the membrane potential of the postsynaptic cell. Depolarization makes the membrane potential more positive and brings the neuron closer to firing an action potential, typically through the movement of ions such as Na+ into the cell. This produces an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP). In contrast, hyperpolarization makes the membrane potential more negative and moves the neuron further from firing, often through the movement of ions such as Cl- into the cell or K+ out of the cell. This produces an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP).

Neurotransmitters can also influence processes throughout the body. For example, acetylcholine triggers muscle contraction at the neuromuscular junction, dopamine regulates movement and reward pathways, and norepinephrine contributes to the stress response, attention, and alertness.

There are many different types of neurotransmitters, each with distinct roles in regulating brain and body functions. The type of neurotransmitter a neuron releases depends on its specific function and classification. Most neurons primarily release one main neurotransmitter; for example, dopaminergic neurons release dopamine, glutamatergic neurons release glutamate, and GABAergic neurons release GABA.

02 · Reference

Glossary

Key terms for chemical communication and receptor-based signaling.

Synapse (synaptic cleft)
The microscopic gap between the presynaptic neuron and postsynaptic cell where neurotransmitters are released and transmitted.
Presynaptic neuron
The neuron that releases neurotransmitters into the synapse.
Postsynaptic neuron
The neuron or target cell that receives and binds neurotransmitters.
Receptors
Proteins located on the surface or inside of cells that bind specific molecules, such as neurotransmitters, hormones, or drugs, to produce a cellular response.
Excitatory neurotransmitter
A neurotransmitter that increases the likelihood that a postsynaptic neuron will generate an action potential by promoting neuronal activation.
Inhibitory neurotransmitter
A neurotransmitter that decreases the likelihood that a postsynaptic neuron will generate an action potential by reducing neuronal activity.
Neuromodulator
A chemical messenger that modifies the activity of neurons or neural circuits rather than directly causing rapid excitation or inhibition.
Agonist
A molecule that binds to a receptor and activates it, mimicking or enhancing the effects of a neurotransmitter.
Antagonist
A molecule that binds to a receptor but blocks or reduces its activation, preventing or decreasing the effects of a neurotransmitter.
Reuptake
The process where neurotransmitters are transported back into the presynaptic neuron after release, allowing them to be recycled or broken down.
Synaptic vesicles
Small membrane-bound structures within the terminal bouton that store neurotransmitters before they are released into the synapse.

03 · Explore

Subtopics

Sections are prepared for the main neurotransmitters and their roles in the nervous system.

Dopamine

Dopamine in the CNS is primarily produced in the midbrain and is separated into three major pathways: the nigrostriatal dopamine pathway, the mesolimbic dopamine pathway, and the mesocortical dopamine pathway.

Dopamine functions as a neuromodulator and is strongly associated with movement, reward, motivation, and attention.

Glutamate

Glutamate is the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system and plays a critical role in neuronal communication, learning, memory, and synaptic plasticity.

The major glutamate receptors include AMPA receptors, which allow Na+ influx and promote depolarization; NMDA receptors, which require both glutamate binding and sufficient depolarization to activate and allow Ca2+ influx; and metabotropic glutamate receptors, which regulate slower and longer-lasting changes in neuronal activity.

Glutamate is central to long-term potentiation. During LTP, repeated activation of glutamatergic synapses strengthens the connection between neurons by increasing the number and responsiveness of AMPA receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.

Excess glutamate activity can be harmful. Excessive stimulation of glutamate receptors, particularly NMDA receptors, can cause excessive Ca2+ influx and neuronal damage, a process known as excitotoxicity.

Glutamate also works closely with GABA, the brain's primary inhibitory neurotransmitter. Both neurotransmitters are derived from glutamine and help maintain a balance between excitation and inhibition.

Key mechanisms:

  • Glutamate → AMPA activation → Na+ influx → depolarization
  • Depolarization removes Mg2+ block → NMDA activation
  • NMDA activation → Ca2+ influx → synaptic strengthening (LTP)
  • Excess glutamate → excitotoxicity → neuronal damage
