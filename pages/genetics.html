Molecular Neuroscience

A structured guide to the genetic and molecular foundations of nervous-system development and function.

01 · Orientation

Overview

Genetics and molecular neuroscience examine how biological information is stored, regulated, and translated into the molecules that build and maintain the nervous system. Genes influence neural development, cellular identity, synaptic function, responses to experience, and vulnerability to disease.

DNA does not determine behaviour in isolation. Gene activity interacts with cellular signals, development, environment, and experience, producing dynamic patterns of protein expression across neurons and glial cells.

02 · Reference

Glossary

Core terms for understanding genetic information and gene expression.

DNA
Deoxyribonucleic acid, the molecule that stores most hereditary information in cells.
RNA
Ribonucleic acid, a family of molecules involved in carrying, regulating, and using genetic information.
Nucleotide
A building block composed of a nitrogenous base, a five-carbon sugar, and a phosphate group.
Gene
A region of DNA that contributes to a functional product, such as a protein or functional RNA.
Chromosome
A packaged DNA molecule containing many genes and regulatory regions.
Codon
A sequence of three nucleotides in messenger RNA that specifies an amino acid or a translation signal.
Transcription
The process of producing an RNA copy from a DNA template.
RNA splicing
Processing that removes introns from pre-mRNA and joins exons to form mature mRNA.
Translation
The process by which ribosomes read mRNA codons and assemble an amino-acid chain.
Protein
A molecule built from amino acids that can provide structure, catalyse reactions, carry signals, or perform other cellular functions.

03 · Explore

Genetic Foundations

From nucleotide sequence to proteins that shape the structure and activity of nervous-system cells.

Genes, DNA, and Chromosomes

Genes can be thought of as biological instructions. Many genes contain the information needed to build proteins, while others produce functional RNA molecules or help regulate when other genes are active.

DNA uses four nucleotide bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), and guanine (G). In double-stranded DNA, A pairs with T and C pairs with G. Human genetic information is organized into chromosomes; most nucleated human cells contain 23 chromosome pairs.

Each nucleotide contains a nitrogenous base, a five-carbon sugar, and a phosphate group. DNA contains deoxyribose and usually uses thymine, while RNA contains ribose and usually uses uracil (U) in place of thymine.

From DNA to RNA to Protein

Protein-coding information in DNA is first copied into pre-messenger RNA through transcription. During RNA processing, introns are removed and exons are joined through splicing, producing mature messenger RNA.

Ribosomes then translate the messenger RNA. They read the sequence in three-nucleotide codons, each of which specifies an amino acid or signals where translation should begin or end. The resulting amino-acid chain folds and may be modified to become a functional protein.

Why Gene Expression Matters in Neuroscience

Neurons and glial cells use gene expression to produce receptors, ion channels, transporters, enzymes, structural proteins, and signalling molecules. Changes in which genes are active can influence development, synaptic plasticity, stress responses, adaptation, and disease processes.
